**The short answer:** choose what to watch before you look anywhere else. Open the full-match video from a link that goes straight to it. Keep away from social feeds, search results and the organiser's short clips until you have watched it.

The rest of this guide goes through each place a result leaks, and what to do about it.

## Where results leak

### The video's length

A full-match video shows its length on the thumbnail, in search results and on the player. A best-of-three that goes to a third map runs clearly longer than one that ends after two. So the length alone can tell you how many maps were played.

**What to do:** don't look for the video on the organiser's channel page or in search results, where every length is on show. Use a link that opens the video itself. The player shows its controls, the time included, while the mouse moves over it: once the video plays, keep the mouse away from the player.

### Chapters

At ESL and PGL, the organisers this site checked in detail, most full-match videos list their chapters in the description: one chapter per map played, often with highlight moments too. The player then splits its progress bar into those chapters. A glance at the bar can tell you how many maps were played, and a chapter's name can tell you what happened in it.

YouTube can also add chapters to a video by itself, and chapters can show in the video's transcript.

**What to do:** don't open the description or the transcript, and don't hover over the progress bar.

### Thumbnails and titles

The organisers' own full-match videos are mostly safe on their own. In the sample this site checked by hand, from ESL and PGL, the thumbnails showed both teams' logos and no score. In the titles this site has seen, the teams and the event are named, sometimes the stage, but not the result.

The risk is the titles of other matches. A semi-final video names its two teams, so it tells you who won the quarter-finals that led to it. A list of uploads shows all of those titles at once.

**What to do:** watch the matches of an event in the order they were played. Don't scroll a channel's list of uploads.

### The organiser's other uploads

At ESL and PGL, the organisers' main channels post short clips that name who was knocked out or who reached the final. Their full matches are on a separate channel, which posts only full matches. StarLadder posts its full matches on its main channel, next to its clips.

**What to do:** don't browse an organiser's channel or its short clips during an event you want to watch later. Open each full match from a link that goes straight to it. If YouTube suggests clips, skip them.

### Recommendations and comments

Next to the video, YouTube suggests other videos. They can be later matches, highlights or clips of the same event, whose titles can give results away. Under the video, viewers comment on the match, and a comment can name the result.

In the last seconds of a video, the player can show other videos too: the next one autoplay would start, and links the channel adds to an end screen.

**What to do:** watch full screen or in theatre mode, and don't scroll below the player. Stop the video once the match is over, before its last seconds.

### Social feeds

Teams, players, organisers and fans post results once a match ends, sometimes in the first line or in the picture. A feed shows them to you without asking.

**What to do:** stay off the feeds until you have watched. Where an app lets you mute words, mute the event's and the teams' names for the days you need.

### Search engines

Searching for a team or an event can show news headlines and video titles that carry the result. Any results box a search engine shows above the links can give the score away too.

**What to do:** don't search for a match you haven't watched yet. Find its video from a link that goes straight to it.

## How this site avoids them

The home page lists the finished matches of PandaScore's top two tiers from the last {{listDays}} days that have an official full-match video. It was built so that nothing it shows gives a result away:

- **Its own card for each match.** A card shows the event, the stage, the date and a label. There is no YouTube thumbnail, no video length, no chapters and no video title.
- **Teams on tap.** The teams stay hidden until you tap "Show teams". A later round's teams would tell you who won the earlier ones.
- **A label, never a result.** The label is given after the match, but it never says who won, the score or how many maps were played. Matches with the same label are ordered by what was at stake, which is known before the match, so the order gives nothing away either.
- **The organiser's own full video.** "Watch the VOD" opens the organiser's full-match video on YouTube.

Once you are on YouTube, the page is YouTube's: the length, the chapters, the comments and the suggestions are all there. The tips above still apply.

How the labels are worked out is in [How "What should I watch?" rates matches](/guides/how-the-watch-rating-works).

## A routine that works

1. Choose your matches on the [home page](/) before you open anything else.
2. Open each one with "Watch the VOD" and press F for full screen (YouTube's shortcut, as of November 2026), rather than clicking the control bar.
3. The control bar, with the time and the progress bar, can show when the video starts or pauses: keep your eyes off the bottom of the screen until it hides, and the mouse away from it. On a phone, a tap on the video can show the same bar, so turn the phone sideways for full screen and don't tap the video once it plays.
4. Watch the matches of an event in the order they were played.
5. Check feeds, search and results only once you are done.
